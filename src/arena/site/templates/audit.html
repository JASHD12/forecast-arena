The decision

An operations analyst reviews tomorrow's 17:00-20:00 (Berlin time) price block. Is it worth raising an alert because the mean evening price will exceed that question's published threshold? A forecast probability is only useful if acting on it beats a simple rule, so two models are compared on decisions as well as on probability scores: the base rate (how often such evenings were expensive before) and a fine-tuned 1.5B language model (stored scores of the open model that runs in this project, with its pinned calibration).

Run it on your own predictions

Two CSV files and a standard-library Python 3.12 script; nothing is uploaded and no key or package is needed. It also counts the days your model had no on-time prediction, and shows what the fallback did to the loss.

python3 examples/forecast-audit/report.py --cases cases.csv --predictions predictions.csv \
  --candidate my_model --baseline reference --false-alert-cost 1 --miss-cost 4 --out report/

The files, the input rules and the outputs are described in the contract; the tool is one file; this case can be rebuilt with its manifest and a SQL cross-check.

What this cannot show

  • The costs are assumed, not an operator's; there is no load profile and no measured saving.
  • These are replayed days that were studied before; the numbers do not predict future performance.
  • The classical gradient-boosted model is not in this comparison: the row-level predictions of its original backtest were not kept. It can be added from a separately identified replay.
  • Data: Bundesnetzagentur | SMARD.de (CC BY 4.0). Only yes/no events and probabilities are shipped, no price series; the licence page says wholesale-price licensing is under review.
